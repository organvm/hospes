## Guest Archetype

An accessibility engineer who treats access as a system property rather than a late review step.
Has built access requirements into routine team decisions — not as a compliance layer added at the
end, but as an executable check owned by a named person at a named gate. Has a public body of
work: open-source tooling, published methodology, or documented organizational change. Is not
primarily a podcast presence; would approach the conversation as a practitioner, not an advocate.

**Why this archetype:** The show's fixed structure (Claim → Stress Test → Artifact) is well-suited
to guests who work with ownership and accountability problems. An accessibility engineer's core
professional problem — "who owns this requirement, who can verify it, and what happens when it
fails downstream?" — maps directly onto the Artifact segment. The compact access receipt the
guest produces in the room is a real working object, not a demonstration.

**Why now:** Austin as a third city extends the show's geographic range and tests a third route
class (C1 inbound) alongside pilot-01 (C1 outreach) and pilot-02 (C2 warm introduction).
Together the three alternate pilots prove the show can source guests across three cities, two
route classes, and three practitioner archetypes.

---

## The Claim

*Accessibility is most durable when teams encode it in routine decisions instead of depending on a
late heroic review.*

---

## Stress Test

Offer a fictional media workflow that passes a launch checklist while losing captions, keyboard
navigation, and correction ownership downstream. Ask the guest to redesign responsibility — who
owns each requirement, what the executable check is, and what the exception route is — without
speaking for any individual's lived experience or implying that any specific disability is in scope.

---

## Artifact

A compact access receipt containing: requirement, accountable owner, executable check, exception
owner, correction route, and publication evidence. Produced in the room; not polished or captioned —
the raw object and the context in which it appeared. The receipt is a working template, not a
reference guide.

---

## Producer Brief

- **Fixed segments:** The Claim, The Stress Test, The Artifact  
- **Rotating segment:** *Object Lesson* — before the conversation starts, place one physical object
  on the table that the guest does not know the purpose of. Fifteen minutes in, ask the guest to
  connect it to something they said in the first half. Tests whether the format generates genuine
  surprise outside a performance frame  
- **Research boundary:** synthetic workflow only; no inferred disability, care preference, or
  personal accommodation requirement  
- **Guest-care boundary:** accommodations are collected directly and kept outside Git; any
  accommodation request or sensitive reply routes to a human owner; never infer preferences from
  the guest's professional field  
- **Approval gate:** any sensitive reply or care request routes to a human owner before any
  follow-up action is taken

## What This Alternate Pilot Tests

**Ownership and accountability structures in a fixed-format conversation.** The accessibility
engineer is the only archetype in either slate whose professional practice is explicitly about
assigning ownership and verifying it. That alignment with the show's core interest — "who owns
this claim, how do we test it, what is the receipt?" — makes this a natural stress test of
whether the Artifact segment can produce a genuinely usable accountability object. If the compact
access receipt is real enough to use, the Artifact segment works. If it is only a demonstration,
the format needs to change before it goes further.
