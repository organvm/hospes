# Segment Architecture

HOSPES uses a fixed three-segment spine for every episode and selects one or two rotating
modules based on the guest. The format should be immediately recognizable without becoming
a game show.

## Why segments matter

Segments are the unit of clips, chapters, social assets, and audience memory. A
conversation that has no designed turns produces one undifferentiated file. A conversation
built around segments produces a discrete editorial object in every act — an episode thesis
from the first segment, conflict and surprise from the second, a shareable artifact from
the third, and a rotating interstitial tuned to each guest's texture. The segment
architecture is also what makes the show identifiable without requiring a celebrity in
every episode: audiences return for the mechanism, not only the guest.

## The three fixed segments

### 1. The Claim

The guest states one consequential proposition: something they believe, something their
field gets wrong, or a decision they would still defend.

Good example: "What do successful comedians understand about audiences that successful
filmmakers still misunderstand?" — a question with a real answer the guest is qualified
to give and the hosts can meaningfully test.

Bad example: "Tell us how you got started." — no proposition, nothing to stress-test,
nothing to produce.

**Output:** episode thesis, title language, and the first major clip.

### 2. The Stress Test

The hosts test the claim through contradiction, counterexample, comedy, research, or a
designed scenario.

Host split: Ari applies instinct, friction, and compression — he is the test of whether
an idea survives ordinary language and skepticism. Anthony applies structure, evidence, and
synthesis — he ensures the friction has something substantial to strike.

**Output:** conflict, surprise, and the strongest conversational clips.

### 3. The Artifact

The guest and hosts produce something that did not exist before the recording: a rule,
prediction, ranking, plan, diagram, short text, question, design, or commitment. Not every
artifact is serious; some are absurd — what matters is that the conversation closes with
a made object rather than a trailing observation.

**Output:** a visual object for the episode, a social asset, and the evidence that
something happened during the recording.

## Eight rotating modules

Choose one or two per episode using the rubric below. A segment earns its slot when it:
exposes a real tension; changes the emotional or intellectual mode; can produce a discrete
clip; gives the guest something interesting to do; does not humiliate or ambush them; and
remains intelligible in audio-only form.

### Receipts

Present a verified quotation, abandoned project, old interview clip, contradictory public
statement, or prediction the guest made on the record. Ask whether it still holds. The
guest should have space to revise, contextualize, or reject the earlier position — this
is not an ambush.

### Object Lesson

The guest brings or selects an object that reveals how they work, think, remember, or
fail. The object anchors the conversation to a physical thing and produces a natural
artifact-camera moment.

**Estate connection:** this segment links directly to Anthony's existing Object Lessons
editorial and multimedia system (documented in `docs/estate-composition.md`). The segment
is one in-show instantiation of a repeatable form that already exists across Anthony's
creative practice — the same object-as-argument method, applied to a guest's world
instead of his own.

### Explain It to Ari

A complex idea must survive compression into plain language and survive Ari's skepticism.
Ari is not framed as the uninformed party; he is the compression test. If the idea cannot
be explained to him, it has not yet been understood by the guest.

### Build the Worst Version

Design the most destructive, absurd, or corrupted version of the guest's industry, idea,
or product. Then identify which parts of that corrupted version already exist. The segment
is productive when the worst version turns out to be recognizable.

### Opposite Chair

The guest must present the strongest version of the position opposite their own — not a
strawman they can easily demolish. The segment is useful when the guest's position is
genuinely controversial and when they are secure enough to inhabit its best opposition.

### Future Headline

The guest writes a plausible headline from five years in the future and explains the
causal chain backward. The exercise is generative, not predictive: it reveals what the
guest believes is already in motion.

### The Unfinished Thing

Revisit a project, belief, relationship, or ambition the guest has not resolved. The
segment is most useful for guests with long careers and public records where the
unfinished thing is already partly visible.

### Question for the Next Guest

The guest leaves one question for a future participant and may name the kind of person —
not necessarily someone they know — who should answer it. This segment quietly compounds
the guest graph without requiring the guest to introduce anyone or the hosts to ask for a
referral.

## Segment selection rubric (full criteria)

A segment earns inclusion only when it:

- exposes a real tension that would not otherwise surface
- changes the emotional or intellectual mode of the conversation
- produces a discrete clip with a clear beginning and end
- gives the guest something genuinely interesting to do
- does not humiliate, ambush, or put the guest in bad faith
- remains completely understandable in audio-only playback
