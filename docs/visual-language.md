# Visual Language — Portable Salon Kernel

## Central rule

**The audience should perceive the room before the production.**

Take the principle from *Club Random* — a room in which a conversation happens — without
copying the décor. The goal is an intimate, intentional encounter that reads as a private
lounge conversation, not a podcast set. The studios in Los Angeles, New York, and Austin
are each different rooms; the visual grammar must travel as a portable kit, not a permanent
installation.

---

## Do not show

- Microphones, boom arms, shock mounts, or headphone clutter
- A desk separating hosts and guest
- Large neon show logo
- Generic RGB wash or colored wall panels
- Symmetrical "creator studio" composition by default
- Rows of acoustic foam panels visible in frame
- Production equipment, cable runs, or branded prop mugs
- Perfectly mirrored two-shot setups

## Use

- Two or three chairs angled toward one another rather than facing the camera
- One low table carrying only objects that have narrative function
- One warm practical lamp visible in frame
- Controlled negative space and a background with actual depth
- One restrained programmable accent or projected texture per episode
- One physical segment deck or artifact box
- One episode-specific object, image, or generated symbol

Minimalism means **few objects with purpose**, not an empty room.

---

## Camera grammar

**1. Environmental master**
Establishes the bodies, the room, and the spatial relation between everyone present.
This shot is the anchor. Shot first; returned to when attention or mode shifts.

**2. Ari single**
Medium or medium-close. Wide enough to preserve enough environment that the frame does
not read as broadcast news or a talking-head interview.

**3. Guest single**
Matched in visual weight to the Ari single. Symmetry in importance, not necessarily
in lens and angle — a perfect mirror read signals production, not encounter.

**4. Artifact camera** (optional)
Overhead or close-detail angle, used only when the episode produces or examines a
physical object — cards, drawings, diagrams, a book, a ranked list, or anything
the Artifact segment generates.

Cuts follow changes in attention, tension, or segment — not every sentence. The edit
may use restrained digital push-ins but should not simulate the pacing of short-form
content.

---

## Hidden-audio grammar

No visible microphones means the audio plan must become **more redundant, not less rigorous**.

- Concealed lavalier on every participant
- Overhead or out-of-frame directional microphone as safety
- Room tone recorded at every setup
- Wardrobe and rustle check before the guest enters
- Isolated tracks for every speaker
- Pre-recording movement test to flag handling noise
- Backup recording path for the full session

Hidden audio is the harder discipline. Every decision that removes a microphone from the
frame must be replaced by a redundant capture path.

---

## Light and palette

- Soft, dimensional face light
- Warm practical source visible in the room
- Darker background with selective depth
- Neutral physical palette: charcoal, bone, wood, aged metal, restrained textile
- One changing signal color or graphic texture per episode

The physical palette is stable across all three studio cities. The signal color changes
by episode and lives primarily in the graphic layer.

---

## Graphic grammar

**The physical room is calm; the graphic layer may become strange.**

This is the doctrine that allows Anthony's multimedia and generative-environment practice
to enter the show without requiring a permanent set renovation.

- Kinetic type rather than persistent lower thirds
- Segment titles appear briefly, then disappear
- Episode-specific glyph, diagram, or artifact derived from the conversation
- Thumbnails describe the episode tension, not merely the guest's name and face
- All three studio cities share typography, pacing, title behavior, and artifact treatment
- One signal color per episode, applied consistently across graphics and packaging

The graphic layer is where the show's visual authorship lives — not in painted walls or
custom furniture.

---

## Field-show grammar (Anthony's GoPro series)

The mobile show should not imitate the flagship with worse equipment. Its distinction is
that the guest's own environment is the third participant.

Use the cameras deliberately rather than trying to approximate studio cinema:

- Wide-angle observational identity — the GoPro's field-of-view is a quality, not a
  compromise; the spatial, environmental quality becomes part of the show's vocabulary
- One environmental master shot of the space
- One guest angle and one host angle
- One roaming or detail camera for objects and demonstrations
- Separate local audio for each participant
- Synchronization slate at the start and after any interruption

The GoPro grammar should read as **deliberate, not apologetic** — the same commitment to
"the audience perceives the room before the production" applied to a workshop, apartment,
studio, archive, or landscape rather than a controlled salon. Do not carry a fake living
room in the suitcase.

---

## v0 → v1 → v2 evolution

No studio renovation at v0. One case per studio city is sufficient.

| Stage | Build | Cost |
|-------|-------|------|
| **v0 — Format proof** | Furniture repositioning, hidden audio, controlled lighting, segment deck, unified graphics | One kit per studio, no construction |
| **v1 — Recognition** | Custom artifact table, repeated sculptural object, replicated lighting package in all three cities | Modest; the format is already proven |
| **v2 — Dedicated environment** | Transformable room whose light, projection, and objects change by episode while preserving the same visual grammar | Only after the show has demonstrated why the environment should evolve |

The v0 constraint is intentional: make a successful show first, then make a dedicated
room. The visual system is designed so that v0 is not a lesser version — it is the proof
that the grammar works without construction.
